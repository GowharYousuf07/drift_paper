\begin{abstract}
Activation-guided methods such as EVA allocate a low-rank adapter's capacity by
profiling the target task's activations beforehand. We test whether this helps
small language models on biomedical classification at matched budgets, with each
configuration tuned separately. Across
eleven methods on three benchmarks, none beats a tuned uniform LoRA by more than
$0.34$ F1 ($1.0$ under HoC's micro-F1), and no difference survives a
multiple-comparison correction. A budget sweep, a $4$M--$110$M ladder, a decoder
SLM and a clinical task agree once multiple comparisons are corrected ($1{,}328$
runs). The methods differ in stability instead, and for activation-PCA
initialisation we trace the cause. On RoBERTa-base, its leading directions
lie on the backbone's outlier dimensions 77 and 588 at 23--24 of the 24 distinct
hidden-state inputs and, over all modules, carry a median $59$--$73\times$ an
average direction's energy, and budget-matched EVA loses 2 of 5 HoC seeds in fp16
and all 3 in fp32. The decoder's signature is even stronger. Whitening these
directions, deflating a general-domain reference subspace (\method{}, which nests
EVA) or the exact generalised-eigenvector contrast largely restore stability
(whitening most reliably) but add no accuracy we can detect at this power. A
random-token reference serves as well as real text, so the reference supplies the
backbone's outliers, not domain knowledge. Neither rank allocation nor placement
helps at a matched budget, and halving the budget costs nothing, so capacity
rarely binds; where it does, no allocation gain survives correction, but our
power is lowest there. We close with a protocol for evaluating allocation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text classification in specialised domains, such as clinical triage,
pharmacovigilance and literature curation, is increasingly deployed under
constraints that rule out large models: patient data cannot leave the
institution, inference must run on commodity hardware, and dozens of
task-specific classifiers must be served concurrently. Small language models
(SLMs), from a few million to a few hundred million parameters, suit this
setting, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) is the natural way to
specialise them. A frozen backbone is shared across tasks, while a small
adapter, typically around $1\%$ of the parameters, carries the task-specific
update~\cite{hu2022lora,han2024peftsurvey}.

\emph{Given a fixed adapter budget, where in the network should the capacity
go?} Uniform allocation, as in standard LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora}, is
budget-oblivious. One family of methods reallocates rank \emph{during} training
by importance scores~\cite{zhang2023adalora,ding2023sora,valipour2023dylora}. A
second, faster family does it \emph{before} training, by profiling activations on
a small calibration set. EVA initialises adapters from the principal directions
of the target activations and redistributes rank by explained
variance~\cite{paischer2025eva}. CorDA~\cite{yang2024corda} and
AIRA~\cite{li2025aira} weight the weight-matrix SVD by an activation covariance,
and TLoRA~\cite{lin2026tlora} and Liu et al.~\cite{liu2026rsprobe} score layers
by task sensitivity or effective rank.

Whether such allocation helps depends on how it is compared. Methods favour
different learning rates, so a baseline that borrows the new method's rate is not
a neutral reference, and moving rank between modules of different width changes
the number of parameters spent, so a matched rank is not a matched budget. Lee
et al.~\cite{lee2026lrmatters} showed that LoRA and nine of its variants reach
similar peak performance on decoder LLMs once every method's learning rate is
tuned. Training-free allocation has not been evaluated under the same protocol,
nor in the regime where it should matter most, namely small backbones adapted to
a specialised domain.

In domain adaptation the question takes a sharper form. Biomedical text is still
English, so its dominant activation directions are largely those the pretrained
model already handles, and profiling a single distribution may spend capacity
where none is needed. Only a contrast between two distributions can reveal
directions that carry target-domain energy \emph{and} are poorly represented in
pretraining. To give activation-guided allocation its best chance, we build that
contrast into an instrument rather than a competing method. \method{}
(Domain-Residual Informed Fine-Tuning) deflates the target activation covariance
by the principal subspace of a general-domain reference corpus, allocates a
global parameter budget over the resulting \emph{drift} spectrum, and initialises
each adapter inside the residual subspace, with two forward passes and no labels
or gradients. Its deflation level $\tau$ contains EVA as the special case
$\tau = 0$, and its exact counterpart, the generalised eigenvectors of the target
and reference covariances, tests the contrast in its principled form.

Allocation should also matter most for small models: the intrinsic dimension of
fine-tuning grows as models shrink~\cite{aghajanyan2021intrinsic}, so a smaller
backbone has less slack to absorb a misallocated budget, and we test this
prediction on a controlled parameter ladder. The study asks four questions.
\textbf{RQ1:} Under matched parameter budgets, with every configuration tuned on
its own, does any allocation or initialisation scheme beat uniform LoRA?
\textbf{RQ2:} Why is activation-PCA initialisation fragile, and does removing
the cause help? \textbf{RQ3:} Which design choice matters (rank allocation,
initialisation or placement), and does placement still matter when the budgets
are matched? \textbf{RQ4:} Does the answer change with the backbone's size or
architecture?

\noindent\textbf{Findings.} This is an evaluation study, and its contributions
lie in its findings. Under matched budgets and per-configuration tuning, no
method beats LoRA by more than $0.34$ F1, no difference with LoRA survives a Holm
correction, and the methods differ in stability rather than accuracy. On
RoBERTa-base, the leading directions of activation PCA lie on the backbone's
known outlier dimensions. Whitening, reference deflation and the generalised
eigenvectors restore most of the stability, none turns it into accuracy, and
random tokens serve as well as WikiText as the reference. Rank allocation alone
slightly hurts, adapter placement changes nothing reliable at a matched budget,
and half the budget matches the full one. On the BERT ladder no gain over LoRA
survives correction, and EVA's large deficit on small backbones at a rate tuned
for RoBERTa-base shrinks to at most $2.5$ points at its own rate. A decoder SLM
and clinical notes repeat the pattern. Along the way, we provide \method{}, a
training-free two-distribution profiler that nests EVA, and a protocol for
evaluating adapter allocation (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}).

\section{Related Work}

\noindent\textbf{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning and its evaluation.}
Adapter bottlenecks~\cite{houlsby2019adapters}, prompt and prefix
tuning~\cite{lester2021prompt,li2021prefix}, bias tuning~\cite{benzaken2022bitfit}
and rescaling vectors~\cite{liu2022ia3} all restrict the update to a small
subspace. LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} dominates because its update merges into the
base weights; DoRA~\cite{liu2024dora}, VeRA~\cite{kopiczko2024vera} and
QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} refine it. Lee et al.~\cite{lee2026lrmatters}
re-evaluate nine LoRA variants with a separate learning-rate search for each,
find similar peak performance on decoder LLMs and trace the differences in their
optimal rates to the largest eigenvalue of the loss Hessian, while Biderman
et al.~\cite{biderman2024lorlearnsless} document the gap that remains to full
fine-tuning in domains far from pretraining. We extend the first kind of study to
training-free rank allocation, matched parameter budgets and small encoders, and
add a mechanistic account of the failure we observe.

\noindent\textbf{Rank allocation and spectral initialisation.}
AdaLoRA~\cite{zhang2023adalora} prunes singular triplets by a sensitivity score
during training, SoRA~\cite{ding2023sora} gates ranks with a sparsifying penalty
and DyLoRA~\cite{valipour2023dylora} trains nested ranks; all need a training run.
Training-free allocation is more recent. EVA~\cite{paischer2025eva} redistributes
rank by explained variance ratio, AIRA~\cite{li2025aira} by layer-wise outlier
ratios, TLoRA~\cite{lin2026tlora} by a sensitivity metric over $W\Sigma_x$, and
Liu et al.~\cite{liu2026rsprobe} by effective rank plus a Fr\'echet distance
under virtual perturbation, all from statistics of the target task alone.
PiSSA~\cite{meng2024pissa} initialises from the leading singular directions of
$W$, MiLoRA~\cite{wang2025milora} from the trailing ones and
LoRA-GA~\cite{wang2024loraga} from the first task gradient, and
CorDA~\cite{yang2024corda} orients the decomposition of $W$ by an input
covariance and, in its knowledge-preserving mode, uses a second distribution to
decide what to freeze, but to our knowledge \method{} is the first to
\emph{allocate rank} by a contrast between two distributions.

\noindent\textbf{Where and at what scale to adapt.}
Which matrices to adapt goes back to LoRA itself~\cite[Sec.~7.1]{hu2022lora}.
He et al.~\cite{he2022unified} find that modifying feed-forward representations
uses added parameters more effectively than modifying attention. Our placement
results test that finding under matched budgets and separately tuned learning
rates. rsLoRA argues that $s = \alpha/r$ shrinks updates too fast as the rank
grows and proposes $\alpha/\sqrt{r}$~\cite{kalajdzievski2023rslora}, and LoRA+
gives $A$ and $B$ different learning rates~\cite{hayou2024loraplus}. We keep
$\alpha/r$, as EVA does, and tune the learning rate at every rank instead.

\noindent\textbf{Domain adaptation and outlier dimensions.}
Continued pretraining~\cite{gururangan2020dapt} and domain
pretraining~\cite{gu2021pubmedbert,lee2020biobert,beltagy2019scibert} improve
biomedical classification but update all parameters; we instead spend a small
adapter budget on a frozen general-domain backbone. Contrasting the second-order
statistics of two domains is classical: CORAL aligns
covariances~\cite{sun2016coral}, subspace alignment aligns principal
subspaces~\cite{fernando2013subspace}, and common spatial patterns take the
generalised eigenvectors of two covariances~\cite{koles1990csp,ramoser2000csp}.
A handful of hidden dimensions in pretrained transformers carry very large
activations, and disabling them badly damages BERT and
RoBERTa~\cite{kovaleva2021bertbusters}. For RoBERTa-base they are dimensions 77
and 588, whose magnitude tracks token frequency~\cite{puccetti2022outliers} and
position~\cite{luo2021positional}. Such rogue dimensions dominate similarity
measures unless the representation is standardised~\cite{timkey2021rogue},
complicate quantisation~\cite{bondarenko2021quant,dettmers2022int8} and appear as
massive activations in LLMs~\cite{sun2024massive}. We show that they capture the
leading directions of activation-PCA initialisation; the whitening we test is
the adapter-space analogue of the standardisation remedy.

\section{Activation-Guided Allocation}
\label{sec:method}

Let the backbone contain adaptable linear modules $m = 1,\dots,M$ with weights
$W_m \in \R^{d^{\mathrm{out}}_m \times d^{\mathrm{in}}_m}$. LoRA replaces $W_m$
by $W_m + s\, B_m A_m$ with $A_m \in \R^{r_m \times d^{\mathrm{in}}_m}$ and
$B_m \in \R^{d^{\mathrm{out}}_m \times r_m}$, so module $m$ consumes $r_m c_m$
parameters with $c_m = d^{\mathrm{in}}_m + d^{\mathrm{out}}_m$. Given a global
budget $B$ we choose the ranks subject to $\sum_m r_m c_m \le B$ and the
initialisation of each $A_m$. When ranks are redistributed, we keep
$s = \alpha/r$ for every module, as EVA's reference implementation does.

\subsection{Activation PCA and a reference contrast}

For a linear module $y = Wx$, every gradient-descent update that starts from
$\Delta W = 0$ has its row space in the span of the inputs $x_i$, so the
input-side factor $A$ should lie there, and a covariance argument tells us which
$r$ directions to pick.

\begin{proposition}[Optimal rank-$r$ input subspace]
\label{prop:pca}
Let $\Delta W^{\star} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_i x_i^{\top}$ with $\delta_i$
zero-mean, isotropic ($\E[\delta\delta^{\top}] = \sigma^2 I$) and independent of
$x_i$, and let $P = A^{\top}A$ be the orthogonal projector onto the row space of
a rank-$r$ factor $A$. Then
\begin{equation}
\E\bigl\|\Delta W^{\star}(I-P)\bigr\|_F^2
   = \sigma^2 d^{\mathrm{out}} N \, \tr\!\left[(I-P)\,\Sigma_x\right],
\end{equation}
with $\Sigma_x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i x_i^{\top}$, and the minimiser over
rank-$r$ projectors is the projector onto the top-$r$ eigenvectors of
$\Sigma_x$~\cite{eckart1936approximation}.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:pca} justifies activation-PCA
initialisation~\cite{paischer2025eva}. We apply it to the \emph{centred} inputs,
as EVA's reference implementation does, since the mean contributes only a
constant offset and in RoBERTa is dominated by a few outlier
features~\cite{kovaleva2021bertbusters,puccetti2022outliers}. The proposition
optimises the \emph{total} gradient energy, however, which is dominated by
directions where $\|x\|$ is simply large, and these need not be domain-specific.

Adaptation is driven by the \emph{systematic} part of the gradient,
$M = \E_D[\delta x^{\top}]$. For module $m$, let $P^{G}_m$ be the orthogonal
projector onto the top-$k_m$ eigenspace of the covariance $\Sigma^{G}_m$ of a
general-domain corpus $G$ (below we drop the index $m$ where a single module is
meant). We assume
\begin{equation}
\text{(A)}\qquad \E_D\bigl[\delta\,(P^{G}x)^{\top}\bigr] \approx 0 ,
\end{equation}
that is, the pretrained module is near-stationary on inputs confined to the
reference subspace. (A) is a modelling assumption, not a derived result. It
gives $\operatorname{row}(M) \subseteq \operatorname{range}(I - P^{G})$, and
applying Proposition~\ref{prop:pca} within that range makes the optimal input
basis the leading eigenbasis of the \emph{drift covariance}
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\Sigma} \;=\; (I - P^{G})\,\Sigma^{D}\,(I - P^{G}).
\label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
No public corpus is the pretraining distribution, so (A) holds only
approximately; we treat it as motivation and test its consequence
(Section~\ref{sec:outliers}).

\subsection{One contrast, three approximations}
\label{sec:family}

Three ways of taming the target's dominant directions appear in this paper, and
all three approximate one operation: whitening the target covariance by a
reference covariance $R$ and taking its leading directions, i.e.\ solving
$\Sigma^{D} v = \mu\, R\, v$. With $R = \Sigma^{G}$ the leading $v$ maximise the
ratio of target to reference energy. This is the criterion of common spatial
patterns~\cite{koles1990csp,ramoser2000csp} and of Fisher's discriminant, and it
suppresses directions that are energetic in both distributions, such as a
backbone's outlier dimensions. \method{}'s deflation replaces $(\Sigma^{G})^{-1}$
by a hard step, with zero weight on the reference's top-$k$ eigenspace and unit
weight elsewhere. Whitened EVA keeps the target's own principal directions and
rescales each to the energy of an average direction, i.e.\ it whitens by the
target covariance itself. We also run the exact criterion, \textbf{GEV}, in
which each $A_m$ holds the leading generalised eigenvectors of
$(\Sigma^{D}_m, \Sigma^{G}_m)$, with $\Sigma^{G}_m$ shrunk towards a scaled
identity~\cite{ledoit2004shrinkage} and uniform rank, so that only the subspace
differs from LoRA.

\subsection{Budgeted allocation and initialisation}
\label{sec:alloc}

Let $\widetilde{\lambda}_{m,1} \ge \widetilde{\lambda}_{m,2} \ge \cdots$ be the
eigenvalues of $\widetilde{\Sigma}_m$, and let
$\hat{\lambda}_{m,i} = \widetilde{\lambda}_{m,i}/\tr(\Sigma^{D}_m)$ be the
scale-free fraction of module $m$'s target-domain energy that direction $i$
captures outside the reference subspace. We allocate by maximising total captured
drift,
\begin{equation}
\max_{\{r_m\}} \ \sum_{m=1}^{M} \sum_{i=1}^{r_m} \hat{\lambda}_{m,i}
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{m=1}^{M} r_m c_m \le B,\ \ 0 \le r_m \le r_{\max},
\label{eq:knapsack}
\end{equation}
a separable concave resource-allocation problem that greedy marginal analysis
solves to within the value of one rank unit (Proposition~\ref{prop:greedy}).
Whether that objective tracks accuracy is the empirical question of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. $P^{G}_m$ spans the fewest leading eigenvectors of
$\Sigma^{G}_m$ that capture a fraction $\tau$ of its trace; each module with
$r_m > 0$ receives the leading eigenvectors of $\widetilde{\Sigma}_m$ as $A_m$
and $B_m = 0$, so the base function is preserved exactly. At $\tau = 0$ nothing
is deflated and \method{} is EVA, allocation by explained variance with
initialisation from the principal directions~\cite{paischer2025eva}. This
nesting attributes any difference from EVA to the deflation alone, and it is what
makes \method{} useful as an instrument even where it is not useful as a method
(Section~\ref{sup:method}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\noindent\textbf{Tasks.}
We use three biomedical benchmarks that differ in granularity, label structure
and size. \textbf{ChemProt} is 13-way chemical--protein relation classification
of PubMed sentences~\cite{krallinger2017chemprot} in the split of Gururangan et
al.~\cite{gururangan2020dapt}, lexically the furthest from general English.
\textbf{RCT-20k} is 5-way rhetorical-role classification of sentences in trial
abstracts~\cite{dernoncourt2017rct}, whose signal lies in discourse structure
rather than terminology. \textbf{HoC} is 10-label classification of abstracts by
hallmark of cancer~\cite{baker2016hoc}, rebuilt at document level so that it
reproduces the BLURB split~\cite{gu2021pubmedbert} ($1{,}295$ / $186$ / $371$
documents). HoC is scored with example-based F1 (micro-F1 where it allows a
comparison with published numbers), the other two with micro-F1. A clinical task
is introduced with its results (Section~\ref{sec:clinical}), and
Section~\ref{sup:setup} gives every split and setting.

\noindent\textbf{Backbones.}
RoBERTa-base (125M)~\cite{liu2019roberta} is our primary backbone and lets us
compare our numbers with published full fine-tuning results on the same
splits~\cite{gururangan2020dapt}. For the scale study we use the BERT miniatures
of Turc et al.~\cite{turc2019wellread}, from BERT-Tiny (4.4M) to BERT-Base
(110M), which share one vocabulary, pretraining corpus and recipe, so only depth
and width vary. For the architecture check we use the decoder-only
SmolLM2-360M~\cite{benallal2025smollm2}, classifying from its last token with a
linear head and adapting all $224$ linear modules.

\noindent\textbf{Adapter placement and budget.}
All low-rank methods adapt every linear map in every transformer block, giving
$M = 72$ modules for RoBERTa-base. The budget is that of uniform rank $8$,
$B = 1{,}327{,}104$ adapter parameters ($1.07\%$ of the backbone's $124.06$M).
\method{} and EVA redistribute rank but never exceed $B$. DoRA ($1.41$M) and
AdaLoRA (up to $1.43$M, after holding $1.5\times$ that during training) spend
slightly more, which, if anything, favours them, and we report the parameters
actually spent. Every method, including linear probing and BitFit, also trains
the backbone's classification head ($0.60$M parameters), which is identical
across methods and excluded from the budget and from every parameter count we
report. \method{} uses $\tau = 0.95$, a per-module cap $r_{\max} = 2r$ (as EVA)
and relative scoring.

\noindent\textbf{Methods.} We compare full fine-tuning, linear probing, BitFit
(all biases)~\cite{benzaken2022bitfit}, LoRA at uniform rank~\cite{hu2022lora},
DoRA~\cite{liu2024dora}, PiSSA~\cite{meng2024pissa} and
AdaLoRA~\cite{zhang2023adalora}. EVA~\cite{paischer2025eva} follows its
reference code with one change: its greedy step spends our parameter budget $B$
rather than a count of rank units, so that it competes under exactly the same
budget. We call this variant \emph{EVA (budget-matched)}, or EVA for short, and
also run EVA's own rank-unit rule. Because EVA proved unstable, we add its
documented whitening option in a scale-normalised form, which rescales each
principal row $u_i$ by $\sqrt{\bar\lambda/\lambda_i}$ with
$\bar\lambda = \tr(\Sigma^{D})/d^{\mathrm{in}}$, so that only its
\emph{direction} differs from a random initialisation. Finally, we run \method{}
and GEV. Every low-rank method uses $\alpha = 16$, no adapter dropout, and,
unless it prescribes its own initialisation, $A$ drawn Kaiming-uniform and
$B = 0$. All methods share one codebase (PyTorch~2.10,
Transformers~5.0~\cite{wolf2020transformers}), data pipeline, optimiser and
evaluation.

\noindent\textbf{Training and tuning.} We use AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw}
with linear warmup and decay, fp16 autocast with dynamic loss scaling (the T4 has
no bf16), and select the epoch by dev-set score. Every configuration from which
we draw a conclusion has its own learning rate, selected on the dev set with seed
$1$ from a grid that is extended by one half-decade step past whichever edge
holds the best dev score until the selection is interior
(Section~\ref{app:lr} lists all $119$). This covers every method on every task,
every LoRA placement and budget, every deflation level, every backbone of the
ladder, the decoder, GEV and the clinical task, and variants that serve only as
controls inherit their parent configuration's rate. $\tau = 0.95$ was fixed
before any experiment and never tuned. The test set is never used for any choice.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting.}
We use three seeds, and five for LoRA, EVA, whitened EVA and \method{}, the four
methods on which the analysis turns, and for the reference controls. We report
the mean and the sample standard deviation and compare methods with a paired
$t$-test over shared seeds, correcting with Holm's method~\cite{holm1979} within
each family of comparisons. A run is counted as \emph{failed} when its best dev
score is more than $10$ points below the median of LoRA's runs on the same task.
The study comprises $1{,}328$ fine-tuning runs, $443$ of them for learning-rate
selection, each on one NVIDIA T4.

\noindent\textbf{Availability.}
All datasets are public. The code, the experiment plans, every per-run result
file (with its per-epoch logs, where recorded) and the profile summaries are
available at \url{https://github.com/GowharYousuf07/drift_paper}, together with
the supplementary material, whose sections, tables and figures are numbered with
an S. The clinical notes come from MTSamples, whose terms allow its sample
reports to be shared for educational use with credit to the site. The Hugging
Face release we use states no licence, so we redistribute no note text, and our
code downloads it from that release.

\subsection{RQ1: tuned LoRA is not beaten}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the main results. Our training harness
reproduces both published reference points available for these splits. Full
fine-tuning of RoBERTa-base reaches $81.7\pm0.4$ micro-F1 on ChemProt, against
$81.9\pm1.0$ reported by Gururangan et al.~\cite{gururangan2020dapt}, and
$80.0\pm2.4$ micro-F1 on HoC, against $79.7$ reported for RoBERTa-base on the
BLURB split~\cite{gu2021pubmedbert}.

\noindent\textbf{No method beats uniform LoRA.} At a matched budget of
$1.07\%$ of the backbone, no method improves on LoRA by more than $0.34$ F1 on
any task (BitFit on HoC, $p = 0.52$). After Holm correction over the $24$
comparisons with LoRA, no difference in either direction is significant, and
every adjusted $p$ is $1.00$. Only one difference reaches $p < 0.05$
uncorrected, and it is a loss: BitFit on ChemProt ($-1.1$, $p = 0.04$). We read
it, like every nominal result in this paper, as suggestive only. With three to
five seeds, the seed-level tests rule out large gains but do not establish
equivalence. Instance-level intervals from a replication of every
parameter-efficient run (Section~\ref{app:ci}) go further. On ChemProt and
RCT-20k every interval ends below $+1.2$ points, so larger gains over LoRA are
excluded, and $12$ of the $16$ lie within $\pm2$ points, which establishes
equivalence at that margin (one we did not fix in advance); the other four
extend past it only on the side of a loss. On HoC none ends above $+3.4$. GEV,
the exact contrast, is within $0.3$ points of LoRA on every task and fails no run.

\IfFileExists{tab_fp32.tex}{\input{tab_fp32}}{}

\noindent\textbf{Stability, not accuracy, separates the methods.} HoC, with
$1{,}295$ training documents, $512$-token inputs and ten labels, is where the
methods differ, and the difference lies in failed runs. At its tuned learning
rate, PiSSA fails in two of three seeds, DoRA in one, EVA in two of five and
\method{} in one of five, while LoRA, whitened EVA, AdaLoRA, BitFit and full
fine-tuning never fail. Rerunning the column in fp32 at the same rates
(Table~\ref{tab:fp32}) separates two explanations. The four fp16 failures of
PiSSA, DoRA and \method{} disappear: none of their nine fp32 runs fails,
including every seed that failed in fp16. EVA's failures do not. All three of its
fp32 seeds, including the two that trained in fp16, collapse early to predicting
no label at all, through its initialisation or that initialisation's interaction
with the randomly initialised head (Section~\ref{sup:rq1}). Across both
precisions, the four initialisations line up with the median energy of their
initial directions over all modules (Section~\ref{sec:outliers}): whitened EVA
($1\times$ by construction) fails nothing, \method{} ($1.5$--$2.7\times$) one
fp16 run and no fp32 run, GEV ($8$--$15\times$) no fp16 run and one fp32 run,
and EVA ($59$--$73\times$) two of five in fp16 and all three in fp32.

\noindent\textbf{Selection at the edge of stability.} On a task this small,
single-seed learning-rate selection lands near the edge of stability, most often
for methods that start from a data-derived subspace. Wherever we checked the
alternative (EVA on HoC in fp32, AdaLoRA on RCT-20k and feed-forward-only rank
$14$ on HoC), selection by the mean over seeds picks the more stable rate. For
EVA it removes its failures but leaves it $2.7$ points below LoRA
(Section~\ref{sup:rq1}). We keep single-seed selection for every table entry, as
Section~\ref{sec:setup} specifies, and report the resulting runs as they are
instead of re-selecting after seeing test scores, but the protocol of
Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol} recommends the multi-seed rule.

\noindent\textbf{EVA's initialisation is the fragile part.} Initialised along
the target's principal directions, EVA (budget-matched) trails LoRA on ChemProt
with four times LoRA's seed standard deviation ($81.5\pm1.2$ vs.\ $82.5\pm0.3$,
five seeds) and falls to $59.4\pm25.4$ on HoC. Whitening its initial directions
removes the HoC failures ($77.3\pm1.3$, none in five seeds). The problem thus
lies in the energy of the chosen directions, not in the rank allocation, and
Section~\ref{sec:outliers} identifies what those directions are.

\subsection{RQ2: why activation-PCA initialisation is fragile}
\label{sec:outliers}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig_outliers.pdf}
\caption{Input energy along each module's leading initial direction, relative
to an average direction (log scale; RoBERTa-base, ChemProt). One point per input
site: $W_Q$, $W_K$ and $W_V$ read the same block input. Filled: EVA's leading
direction peaks on outlier dimension $77$ or $588$; hollow circles: the
attention-output and feed-forward hidden inputs, which are not in the
hidden-state basis. Open triangles: \method{} after deflation; crosses: GEV.}
\label{fig:outliers}
\end{figure}

For most RoBERTa-base modules, the leading principal direction of the input
covariance is one of the backbone's two known outlier dimensions rather than a
direction of biomedical meaning (Fig.~\ref{fig:outliers}). In the modules that
read the hidden state ($W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$ and the feed-forward up-projection),
the leading direction peaks on dimension $588$ or $77$. This happens at all $24$
distinct hidden-state inputs on ChemProt and at $23$ of $24$ on RCT-20k and HoC.
These directions carry a median $56$--$71\times$ the input energy of an average
direction, and over all $72$ modules the median is $59$--$73\times$, the
aggregate we quote from here on unless we name a module group. An adapter whose
input-side factor points along such a direction sees input energy one to three
orders of magnitude larger than a randomly initialised one, so a learning rate
that is comfortable for LoRA becomes a very large effective step: in the terms
of~\cite{lee2026lrmatters}, curvature rises and the usable learning rate falls.
This matches three observations. EVA fails on HoC, where single-seed tuning
selected LoRA's rate for it; at ranks $1$ and $2$, where the fixed scale
$\alpha/r$ is largest, it collapses already at $3\times10^{-4}$, whereas LoRA,
whitened EVA and \method{} still train at $10^{-3}$; and it recovers under
whitening, which shrinks these rows. The selected learning rates are weaker
evidence, since they separate EVA from the low-energy initialisations only on
ChemProt (Section~\ref{sup:rq2}).

The general-domain reference carries the same outlier dimensions, which are a
property of the backbone and not of biomedical text, so the reference subspace
absorbs them and deflation removes them: \method{}'s leading drift directions
carry a median of only $1.5$--$2.7\times$ average energy. What the reference
removes first is therefore not ``general English'' in a linguistic sense, as our
motivating argument assumed, but the backbone's own idiosyncratic high-energy
dimensions.

\noindent\textbf{The reference corpus matters only through the backbone.} With
news articles, word-shuffled WikiText or uniformly random tokens as the
reference, the reference subspace still absorbs the outlier dimensions, and the
leading directions carry a median $3$--$4\times$ the energy of an average
direction with news or shuffled text and $13$--$15\times$ with random tokens,
against $1.5$--$2.7\times$ with WikiText. Accuracy follows none of these
differences (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). No reference differs significantly from
WikiText ($p \ge 0.07$), and the shuffled reference's gain over LoRA on ChemProt
($+0.6$, $p = 0.0007$ uncorrected) rested on three nearly identical paired
differences: over five seeds the gain shrinks to $+0.4$ ($p = 0.05$
uncorrected; $p = 0.24$ after Holm correction over the five reference-versus-LoRA
comparisons). A reference made of random tokens is thus as good as real text:
what the contrast contributes is the backbone's outlier structure, which any
input exposes, not knowledge of general English.

\noindent\textbf{The exact contrast gives the same outcome.} GEV's leading
directions avoid the outlier dimensions at all $24$ hidden-state inputs and carry
a median $8$--$15\times$ average energy (Fig.~\ref{fig:outliers}), and GEV is
within $0.3$ points of LoRA on every task ($p \ge 0.72$). Deflating even half of
the reference energy removes what matters (Fig.~\ref{fig:tau}): the robust
effect of the whole family is the collapse of the seed scatter rather than a gain
in the mean, and no member carries accuracy past LoRA.

\subsection{RQ3: allocation or initialisation?}
\label{sec:ablation}

\IfFileExists{tab_ablation.tex}{\input{tab_ablation}}{}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} factorises the design space. \method{} changes two
things at once, namely which modules get rank and which subspace each adapter
starts in, so we vary the two independently on all three tasks. Combining
\method{}'s rank allocation with LoRA's random initialisation scores lower than
uniform LoRA in every seed on ChemProt ($-0.3$, $p = 0.03$ uncorrected; like the
other nominal results, suggestive only) and is destabilising on HoC, where one of
its three seeds fails ($65.0\pm25.1$). \method{}'s initialisation with uniform
ranks is the best low-rank configuration in the upper block on ChemProt
($82.9\pm0.9$), though not significantly better than LoRA ($p = 0.46$), and it
fails no run. Allocation is thus the half of the method that costs stability,
while initialisation is the half that is free.

Allocation also has little to work with. If target and reference shared their
geometry exactly, a fraction $1-\tau$ of the target variance would fall outside
the reference subspace by construction. Averaged over the $48$ distinct input
sites, the excess over $1-\tau$ is only $2.5$ (ChemProt), $2.2$ (RCT-20k) and
$1.4$ (HoC) points of variance at $\tau = 0.95$, and divergences that do not
depend on $\tau$ agree (Section~\ref{sup:rq3}): biomedical text largely reuses
the geometry of general English. The visibly different allocation that
\method{} produces yields the same accuracy as uniform rank.

\subsection{RQ3: where should the adapter go?}
\label{sec:placement}

\IfFileExists{tab_placement.tex}{\input{tab_placement}}{}

Allocation moves rank between modules that all carry an adapter, whereas
placement decides which modules carry one at all. Adapting only the feed-forward
matrices at rank $8$ spends $0.74$M parameters instead of $1.33$M, so a
comparison with all-module rank $8$ confounds placement with budget.
Table~\ref{tab:placement} therefore compares each placement with another at the
same budget, with every configuration tuned on its own: feed-forward-only rank
$14$ ($1.29$M) against all-module rank $8$ ($1.33$M), and all-module rank $4$
($0.66$M) against feed-forward-only rank $8$ ($0.74$M).

\noindent\textbf{At matched budgets.} Neither pair shows a reliable difference.
Against all-module rank $8$, feed-forward-only rank $14$ is $+0.3$ on ChemProt
($p = 0.52$) and $-0.2$ on RCT-20k ($p = 0.53$). On HoC, two of its three seeds
fail at the single-seed rate, and at the rate the three-seed dev mean selects it
is $0.9$ points below all-module rank $8$ ($p = 0.78$) and $1.0$ below
feed-forward-only rank $8$. Against feed-forward-only rank $8$, all-module rank
$4$ is $-0.8$ on ChemProt ($p = 0.10$), $-0.2$ on RCT-20k ($p = 0.65$) and $+0.7$
on HoC ($p = 0.07$). The feed-forward advantage of He et
al.~\cite{he2022unified} is therefore not a placement effect that survives budget
matching here. Nor are fewer parameters an advantage: all-module rank $4$
($0.66$M) differs from all-module rank $8$ by $+0.1$, $-0.4$ and $+0.8$ points
($p \ge 0.34$), and attention-only rank $8$ ($0.59$M) by at most $0.2$. At this
budget and below it, capacity is not what limits accuracy. \method{} placed on
one module type, at the rate it inherits from all-module \method{}, is never
significantly better than LoRA placed there (Section~\ref{sup:rq3}).

\subsection{RQ4: budget, scale and architecture}
\label{sec:scale}

\IfFileExists{figs/fig_budget.pdf}{%
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{figs/fig_budget.pdf}
\caption{Test micro-F1 against adapter budget (uniform-equivalent rank) on
ChemProt, mean$\pm$s.d.\ over three seeds, every (method, budget) pair at its own
tuned learning rate (Section~\ref{app:lr}).}
\label{fig:budget}
\end{figure}}{}

\IfFileExists{tab_ladder.tex}{\input{tab_ladder}}{}

\noindent\textbf{Budget.} Across budgets from rank $1$ to $16$ on ChemProt
(Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}), with the learning rate tuned at every budget, LoRA,
\method{} and whitened EVA stay within $1.0$ points of each other at every
budget, and LoRA is flat from rank $2$ ($82.4$) to rank $16$. Where the budget
binds hardest, at rank $1$, \method{} has the best mean ($81.8\pm0.1$ against
LoRA's $81.0\pm0.4$; $+0.9$, $p = 0.04$ uncorrected, not significant after Holm
correction over the sweep's twelve comparisons with LoRA). This is the one place
in our study where allocation might have room to help. On RCT-20k and HoC at
ranks $4$ and $16$, the ranking matches Table~\ref{tab:main}
(Section~\ref{app:budgetx}). HoC is the one task where the budget itself matters:
LoRA at rank $16$ beats LoRA at rank $8$ by $2.4$ points ($p = 0.05$
uncorrected), and no allocation method beats LoRA at that budget either.

\noindent\textbf{Model scale.} Table~\ref{tab:ladder} tests the prediction that
allocation should matter more as the backbone shrinks, with every method tuned on
every backbone. The prediction is not borne out. LoRA has the best mean on
BERT-Mini, -Small and -Medium, and \method{} on BERT-Tiny ($+0.3$, $p = 0.89$)
and BERT-Base ($+1.3$, $p = 0.03$ uncorrected), the largest gain over LoRA in
the ladder but not significant after Holm correction over its twenty comparisons
with LoRA. At their own tuned rates, EVA trails LoRA by $0.0$ to $2.5$ points and
whitened EVA by at most $1.8$, none significantly. The last column keeps EVA at
the rate tuned for it on RoBERTa-base, as our first version of this study did.
EVA then falls further behind as the backbone shrinks, to $2.7$, $4.9$, $6.9$ and
$13.4$ points behind the tuned LoRA from BERT-Medium down to BERT-Tiny, and this
column's loss on BERT-Mini is the one difference in the ladder that survives
correction. Most of that deficit comes from the inherited learning rate, the
clearest illustration of why a rate tuned for one backbone is not a neutral
choice for another. EVA's remaining deficit grows as the backbone shrinks while
its energy excess falls (Section~\ref{sup:rq4}), so it has another cause, and no
allocation method's difference from LoRA moves in its favour as the backbone
shrinks, so the ladder gives the prediction no partial support either.

\noindent\textbf{A decoder SLM.} SmolLM2-360M has the strongest outlier
signature we measured: one dimension hosts EVA's leading direction at $41$ of
the $64$ hidden-state inputs, consistent with the massive activations reported
for LLMs~\cite{sun2024massive}, and EVA's leading directions carry a median
$125\times$ the energy of an average direction over all $224$ modules. It
reproduces the pattern seen on RoBERTa on ChemProt (Table~\ref{tab:decoder}). No
method beats LoRA ($83.9\pm0.5$), and EVA is again the least stable
($82.2\pm3.6$, one seed at $78.1$). On HoC the cause differs: in two of EVA's
three seeds the fp16 loss scale collapses. Rerun in fp32, EVA trains in every
seed without a non-finite step. It scores $79.8\pm1.7$, level with LoRA's
$80.7\pm1.5$ in fp32 ($-0.9$, $p = 0.66$), so on the decoder EVA's instability is
fp16 overflow rather than an early collapse, and removing it again adds no
accuracy.

\subsection{Clinical notes}
\label{sec:clinical}

To test whether the findings survive the shift towards clinical style, we
classify transcribed clinical notes from MTSamples~\cite{mtsamples} into $12$
specialties (Section~\ref{sup:clinical}). Both the mechanism and the conclusions
carry over. EVA's leading directions peak on dimension $77$ or $588$ at $21$ of
the $24$ hidden-state inputs and carry a median $57\times$ the energy of an
average direction over all $72$ modules, compared with $24$ of $24$ and $73\times$
on ChemProt, and deflation leaves one such input and a median of $4.0\times$.
LoRA is again unbeaten---$84.6$ micro-F1 ($84.3$ macro) against \method{}'s
$84.5$ ($-0.1$, $p = 0.86$), EVA's $83.5$ ($-1.1$, $p = 0.20$) and whitened
EVA's $83.4$ ($-1.2$, $p = 0.13$)---and the random-token reference serves as well
as WikiText (Table~\ref{tab:clinical}). Profiling itself costs less than half a
LoRA training run, its reference pass can be reused across domains, and it adds
no inference cost, since the adapter merges into the base weights
(Section~\ref{sup:cost}).

\section{Discussion: Evaluating Adapter Allocation}
\label{sec:discussion}

Inference cost does not separate the methods we compare. Every low-rank adapter
merges into the backbone after training, so the costs that differ are training
and profiling time, and the only question is whether profiling buys accuracy.
Our results do not say that adapter capacity is irrelevant, since at rank $1$ it
does bind. What they say is that, for small encoders at a budget of about $1\%$,
where the capacity goes (across modules or across module types) matters less
than how each configuration is trained, that is, the learning rate selected for
it and the energy of its initial directions. Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol} condenses
these lessons into a protocol, and Section~\ref{sup:protocol} gives the evidence
behind each step. Our results support the defaults that PEFT libraries ship
(adapters on all linear maps, $s = \alpha/r$, Kaiming-uniform $A$ and $B = 0$)
for small encoders, with two amendments: report the parameters an adapter
actually spends, and make data-derived initialisations report the energy of
their directions.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.94\columnwidth}
\small
\textbf{A protocol for evaluating adapter allocation}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\leftmargin}{1.2em}
\item \emph{Budget in parameters.} Match adapter parameters, not total rank,
      and report the parameters actually spent (head excluded).
\item \emph{Tune every configuration.} Select the learning rate separately for
      every method, backbone, budget and placement, by the mean dev score over
      at least three seeds (on small tasks one seed can land on the edge of
      stability, Section~\ref{sec:main}), and extend each grid until the
      selection is interior.
\item \emph{Seeds and failures.} Use at least five seeds on small tasks. Report
      medians and failed runs next to means, rerun failures in fp32 to separate
      numerics from method, and correct for multiple comparisons.
\item \emph{Energy check.} Before training a data-derived initialisation,
      compute each initial direction's energy relative to an average direction
      (target forward pass plus one leading eigenvector per module). On
      RoBERTa-base and SmolLM2-360M, medians of $59$--$125\times$ coincided with
      failed or unstable runs and $1$--$15\times$ with stable training; on the BERT ladder
      $20$--$59\times$ trained stably at tuned rates. Read a high median as a
      warning to lower the rate, whiten or deflate---not as a verdict.
\item \emph{Check that the budget binds.} Before trying allocation, run LoRA at
      half the budget and at other placements of the same budget. If nothing
      changes, allocation has nothing to exploit. Neither check needs profiling.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}

\section{Limitations}

Three to five seeds detect only large differences: the instance-level intervals
exclude gains above $1.2$ points on ChemProt and RCT-20k, but on HoC only those
above $3.4$. Learning rates are selected on one seed, which on HoC puts several
methods near the edge of stability, and the controls of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} inherit their parent configuration's rate. The MTSamples
notes are sample reports published for medical transcriptionists, not records
from an electronic health record, so they test clinical style rather than
clinical deployment, and because every profile-based method centres its inputs,
a mean shift between domains is invisible to them by construction. Input-side
profiling cannot separate $W_Q$, $W_K$ and $W_V$, which share one block input;
WikiText is only a proxy for the pretraining distribution; architectures without
pronounced outlier dimensions may not show EVA's instability; and larger
decoders, generative formulations and other specialised domains remain untested
(Section~\ref{sup:limits}).

\section{Conclusion}

For practitioners the advice is short. On small encoders at budgets like ours,
use all-module LoRA at rank $4$ to $8$, select its learning rate by the mean dev
score of at least three seeds, check whether a larger budget helps before trying
to allocate one, and skip data-derived allocation. If you do use a data-derived
initialisation, check the energy of its directions and whiten them when it is
high.

Activation-guided allocation asks where the target distribution's variance is
largest. On RoBERTa-base, for most modules, the answer is one of two outlier
dimensions of the backbone, and adapters initialised there are fragile.
Whitening those directions, deflating them with a general-domain reference
(\method{}), or taking the generalised eigenvectors of the target and reference
covariances restores most of the stability without labels, gradients or
inference cost. But under a matched parameter budget, with every configuration
tuned on its own, no such fix and no allocation rule we tested outperformed
uniform LoRA on three biomedical benchmarks, a clinical task or a decoder SLM.
Rank allocation alone slightly hurt, adapter placement changed nothing reliable
at a matched budget, and half the budget was enough. This verdict holds where
the budget does not bind, which at the budgets we tested is almost everywhere.
Where it binds (rank $1$ on ChemProt, and HoC, where rank $16$ beats rank $8$),
no allocation rule gained after correction, but three seeds give us the least
power there, and we tested only input-side, activation-derived signals. On
smaller backbones the learning rate tuned for each backbone, not any allocation
rule, decided how far EVA trailed LoRA.

We believe this is the right baseline for the field to report against.
Improvements claimed for activation-aware allocation should be measured at
matched parameters, against a separately tuned LoRA, at learning rates tuned for
the backbone, budget and placement in question, over enough seeds to reveal
their failures, and checked for the stability of their initialisation. Natural
next steps are to test the outlier mechanism in larger decoders, generative
tasks and real clinical records, and to ask whether a cheap output-side signal
can make allocation matter where input geometry alone does not.
